## B. ISSF — a substantive challenge *(#20, p.27)*

> *"ISSF is tuna-specific. If one sources tuna against the MSC Fishery standard there is no longer a need to source against ISSF requirements… in my view this is not a strategic differentiator — I could see it as a temporary state before MSC, and if sourced for longer it is an excuse not to source MSC."*

You currently place ISSF in **Tier 4, strategic differentiation**. He is saying it belongs earlier in the maturity sequence, not later — a stepping stone rather than a leadership signal.

**This one matters** because ISSF is already a firm-profile opt-in in your matrix, switched *off* for the modelled firm. If you accept his reading, the tier assignment changes but the scoring does not.

**Decision:** keep ISSF in Tier 4, move it, or add a sentence acknowledging the debate.

## E. Table 2 in Chapter 6 — the sharpest technical point *(#22, p.46)*

On your sourcing-claims table:

> *"Nomad is the only one at 99.9% MSC/ASC sourcing. When Thai Union says 98.9% of tuna is MSC, in MSC assessment, or FIP-covered, that can mean 20% MSC, another 10% in assessment, and the remainder FIP… they only make a statement on tuna, not non-tuna, and they do not disclose how much is pure MSC."*

**He is right, and this is the table I helped you build.** The percentages are not comparable: some are certified volume, some bundle certified + in-assessment + improvement projects, and some cover only one species. Presenting them in a single column invites a reading the underlying claims do not support.

**Options:** add a column stating what each figure counts; add a caveat sentence; or drop the percentages and keep the qualitative claims. **I would add the "basis of claim" column** — it makes the table more informative, not less, and it is exactly the kind of precision the chapter argues for elsewhere.

Note his self-interest here: Nomad's figure is the cleanest. That does not make him wrong.
