## 3. E1-7 (GHG removals) and E4-1 (biodiversity transition) — "we did not report either, in either year"

**Taken seriously and written into the thesis as a limitation.** The interoperability index maps GRI 305-5, *reduction* of emissions, to E1-7, which is about *removals* — and a reduction is not a removal. E4-1 is credited through the GRI 304 management approach in the same way.

§5.3 now carries a paragraph describing this **over-crediting** explicitly, using both of your examples, and notes that the mapping is EFRAG's rather than mine, so overriding it case by case would substitute private judgement for a published crosswalk. It also states the consequence honestly: E1-7 is recorded for two of the six firms and E4-1 for four, so neither enters the universal core and no finding depends on either.

## 4. E1-9 — "covered in our TCFD statement for both years"

**Confirmed, and left as coded, deliberately.** Your 2024 GRI index carries GRI 201-2 and points it at the TCFD statement. The 2025 index carries 201-1 but not 201-2, while the TCFD statement itself is still published and linked. So the disclosure exists and the index no longer reaches it.

The coding follows each firm's own index, so it records the index change rather than a disclosure change — which is exactly the methodological point the chapter makes. **One question back to you:** do you know why 201-2 was dropped from the 2025 index? That would turn this from a coding artefact into a finding about indexes drifting from disclosures.

## 8. The cohort-invariance figure — decouple, two green graphs, the "so what"

**All three taken, and the underlying claim turned out to be wrong.** Your instinct to test it was right.

You asked: *"what if you remove another reporter, let's say Nomad — is the cohort size still invariant?"* I ran every leave-one-out and every leave-two-out. The answer is no. The near-universal set is unchanged only when Thai Union is removed; drop any other firm and it moves — 31 without Nomad, 35 without Bolton, 37 without Profand, 38 without Espersen, 41 without Mowi. The reason is mechanical: at a proportional threshold, removing the broadest reporter withdraws mostly affirmative observations, while removing a narrower one withdraws negatives and promotes requirements sitting just below the line.

So the original claim only held for the one firm I happened to test. §5.7 now says this plainly.

**The stronger result is the one underneath it.** The 13-requirement universal core is retained under all six leave-one-out subcohorts and all fifteen leave-two-out subcohorts, and can only grow when narrow reporters are removed. That is a floor beneath any subset of the cohort. The figure was rebuilt as two panels showing exactly that contrast, with the heading you suggested.

## 9. Minimum sample size

**Partly answered by the above.** Removing two of six leaves the core intact, so within this cohort the core is stable down to four firms. What cannot be established from six firms is the general minimum, and the chapter does not claim it.

## 10. "They are super homogenous — the main denominator is fish processing"

**Your sharpest point, and it is now conceded in §5.1 rather than argued around.** The six differ in sourcing model, ownership and regulatory regime, but are alike in the respect that most plausibly drives disclosure: all are processors selling into a common European buyer base, meeting overlapping certification demands and advised by an overlapping professional community.

What the cohort can establish is that firms facing a **common demand environment** converge on a common core. What it cannot establish is that the same core would emerge among firms facing different environments. The first is what the thesis claims, and it is what the institutional argument in Chapter 2 predicts — convergence under shared pressure is the expected outcome, not a surprising one. The value of the core is therefore not that it is unexpected but that it is *identifiable*.

## 11. "Parlevliet & van der Plas are quite big and you did not assess them, correct?"

**Correct, and now stated in §5.2 rather than left out.** They publish a report with a GRI content index, so they satisfy every selection criterion. The cohort was capped at six to keep requirement-level manual coding tractable, and the six were chosen to span the sourcing models and both reporting frameworks — so the cap, not the frame, excluded them.

The consequence is bounded rather than assumed: because the core is retained under every removal of one or two firms and can only widen, a core defined on more firms is no less likely to contain these 13 requirements. A seventh firm could have narrowed the wider near-universal set, which is one reason nothing in the chapter rests on that set.
