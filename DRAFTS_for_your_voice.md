## 1. Comment #10 — the "biased statement" *(angle1_ecosystem.tex, line 182–185)*

**His comment:**
> "This is a biased statement. It comes across as if SMEs would only respond to customer/regulator requests because they have to… the idea behind regulators' and retailers' requests is not to make life difficult, it is to make life more sustainable… I understand your thesis ought to be a helpful tool for SMEs, so it is an invitation to join?"

**Current:**
> By *stakeholder source*, regulators account for 19 instruments and customers for 14 - together two-thirds of the field - while investors (6) and industry or NGO initiatives (10) make up the remainder. What a small processor faces is thus overwhelmingly a regulator-and-customer affair.

The phrase doing the damage is **"what a small processor faces"** — it frames the field as something happening *to* the firm. Combined with "overwhelmingly", it reads as besiegement.

**Proposed:**

> By *stakeholder source*, regulators account for 19 instruments and customers for 14 - together two-thirds of the field - while investors (6) and industry or NGO initiatives (10) make up the remainder. The demand environment is thus overwhelmingly a regulator-and-customer one. That concentration is worth reading carefully: it describes where the demands originate, not a verdict on why they are made. Both channels exist to move the sector toward outcomes the sector itself has largely endorsed, and the retailer requirements in particular often codify what a well-run processor would do regardless. The argument of this thesis is not that these demands are unwelcome, but that answering them one at a time is what makes them costly - and that a firm which builds once against the common core is better placed to treat them as an agenda it participates in rather than a queue it services.

**Why this wording:** it keeps your finding intact, removes the besiegement framing, and connects forward to Chapter 7 — which is where your actual argument lands. Oliver's "is it an invitation to join?" is answered in the last sentence.

## 3. §5.1 — the homogeneity objection *(angle2_cohort.tex, §5.1)*

**His comment:**
> "You defined heterogeneity by wild, aquaculture, multi-species, vertical integration… but actually they are super homogenous as the main denominator is fish processing, and with that as a basis all players face the same stakeholder community and listen to the same advice on what to report on."

**This is his strongest point** and it needs a real answer. §5.1 argues the core is meaningful *because* the firms differ. If they're homogeneous in the way that matters, convergence is much less surprising.

**Two honest routes.** I'd take the second.

**Route A — dispute it.** The cohort spans two regulatory regimes and two reporting frameworks, so "the same stakeholder community" isn't quite right. *Weakness: it's a partial answer, and he'd likely push back that Thai Union still sells into the same EU retailers.*

**Route B — concede and absorb it.** His objection actually supports your mechanism rather than undermining it.

**Proposed (Route B):**

> A qualification is needed on what this cohort's diversity establishes. The six firms differ in sourcing model, ownership and regulatory regime, but they are alike in the respect that most plausibly drives disclosure: all are seafood processors selling into a common European buyer base, subject to overlapping certification demands and advised by an overlapping professional community. A practitioner reviewer put the objection directly - that beneath the sourcing-model differences the cohort is substantially homogeneous. The observation is correct, and it bears on how the convergence result should be read. What the cohort can establish is that firms facing a common demand environment converge on a common disclosure core; what it cannot establish is that this core would emerge among firms facing genuinely different demand environments. The first is what the thesis claims, and it is what the institutional account in Chapter 2 predicts: convergence under shared coercive and normative pressure is the expected outcome, not a surprising one. Read this way the core's value lies not in being unexpected but in being *identifiable* - it marks where the sector's demand environment has already settled, which is precisely what a firm entering that environment needs to know.

**Why:** it turns his objection into support for your theory rather than a wound, and it's more defensible than claiming diversity you don't have. It does soften the "despite their differences" reading of §5.1 — check whether anything downstream leans on that.
