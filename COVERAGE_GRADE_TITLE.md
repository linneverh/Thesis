### New errors found in this pass

**Table 1 (p.29) misdescribed two firms' reporting basis.** The table said Nomad Foods and Profand report under "GRI". Checking their actual reports: both report **"with reference to"** the GRI Standards, not *in accordance with*. Thai Union genuinely reports *in accordance with* (confirmed in both their 2024 and 2025 reports). That distinction is not cosmetic in GRI terms — "with reference to" means citing selected disclosures without claiming full application.

This matters for your method: **with-reference firms supply fewer mappable disclosures by construction**, which partly explains why Nomad and Profand sit mid-table. That's now stated in §6.3 as a reporting-choice effect rather than a performance one. It also pre-empts an obvious reviewer objection from those two firms.

**Nahrstedt (2026) was misdescribed — three times.** The paper is real: Jan Nahrstedt, University of Hamburg, SSRN 6724699, 241 large listed EU companies, FY2024–FY2025. I pulled the abstract.

But it measures **double-materiality outcomes** — how many topics firms *declare material* — not disclosure rates. Your text said it "finds disclosure rates declining." Those are different variables, and in this literature the difference is the sort of thing an accounting examiner spots immediately.

The finding still supports you, and arguably more interestingly: firms aren't just reporting less on environmental topics other than climate, they're increasingly judging them **immaterial**. Corrected in all three places, with the distinction stated explicitly, plus "241 EU firms" → "241 large listed EU companies" (your cohort includes non-listed firms).

## 2. On 5.25 — what the number means

Swiss scale, 1–6. **4.0 is a pass. 5.0 is "good". 6.0 is essentially flawless.** A 5.25 is a solid, comfortably above-average Master's thesis — the band where most genuinely good work lands. Theses that get 5.5+ usually have something extra: a publishable contribution, or an unusually sharp theoretical argument.

I'm not being harsh. I'm saying you have a **5.5 thesis with a 4.5 discussion chapter attached**, and the average is pulling you down.

The evidence for that: Chapter 8 is ~3,000 words containing **four citations**. Barney, Teece, Mitchell, DiMaggio and Suchman — the five lenses Chapter 3 builds — never reappear. You spend a chapter constructing theoretical apparatus and then don't use it to interpret your own results.

## 3. How to enhance — in priority order

**A. Rebuild Chapter 8's front half (2 hours, worth ~0.25–0.5 of a grade point).**

Cut §8.1 from 555 words to ~150. Add a new §8.2 "What these findings mean theoretically", 600–800 words, three moves:

1. **Narrow law, broad cascade → stakeholder salience.** Mitchell et al. say firms triage claimants by power, legitimacy, urgency. You show the *transmission structure* determines what reaches a small firm at all — a regulator with high power over a large customer generates more SME burden than a regulator binding the SME directly. That refines salience theory for value-chain contexts. This is your most publishable idea and it is currently unstated.
2. **The universal core → institutional isomorphism.** You already gesture at DiMaggio & Powell in Chapter 6. Say which mechanism your evidence supports: coercive can't explain it (three firms aren't CSRD-bound the same way), so it's mimetic/normative. Then note what would distinguish them.
3. **Capability sharing → RBV.** Barney's inimitability criterion actually cuts *against* you: if every firm can build traceability data, it isn't a competitive advantage, it's table stakes. Say so. Engaging a theory that doesn't perfectly fit is what distinguishes a 5.5 from a 5.0.

**B. Expand §8.7 from 136 words into a real conclusion** — what is now known that wasn't, what remains open, what you'd tell a practitioner in one paragraph.

**C. Add "so what for the reader who isn't a seafood SME"** — the transferability claim in §1.4 is asserted and never revisited.

**D. If you get cohort responses by 14 August, add a short §6.9** reporting what they said. Even "three firms confirmed, one corrected X" converts your weakest methodological point into a strength.

Do A and B and you're at 5.5. A, B, C and D and you're arguing for higher.

## 4. Title

Current: *Navigating the Sustainability Disclosure Ecosystem: A diagnostic framework for European seafood processing SMEs*

Two problems. "Navigating" and "ecosystem" are both softly generic — a reader can't tell what you found. And it names the method (a diagnostic framework) rather than the result, so the title carries no finding.

Your strongest, most distinctive result is that fragmented demand **converges**, so building a few things first answers most of it. The title should say that.

**My recommendation:**

> **Build Once, Answer Many: Where Disclosure Demand Converges for European Seafood-Processing SMEs**

Says the finding, keeps the sector, and "build once, answer many" is memorable and already your own phrase from Chapter 7.

**Alternatives, depending on emphasis:**

| Emphasis | Title |
|---|---|
| The cascade finding (most academic) | *Narrow Law, Broad Cascade: How Large-Firm Regulation Reaches Small Suppliers, and Where They Should Start* |
| Sequencing / practical | *What to Build First: Converging Sustainability-Disclosure Demand in European Seafood Processing* |
| Empirical frontier framing | *The Disclosure Frontier and the Convergent Core: A Diagnostic for Resource-Limited Seafood Processors* |
| Closest to current, just sharper | *From Reactive to Proactive: Where Sustainability-Disclosure Demand Converges for European Seafood-Processing SMEs* |

If Bonardi is a strategy professor, the first or second will land better than the current one — both foreground a mechanism rather than a description.

One caution: if the title is already registered with the programme, check whether changing it needs approval before you do it.
